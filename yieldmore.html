Project scenario

After IT professionals identify threat/vulnerability pairs and estimate the likelihood of their occurrence, IT management must decide which risk management techniques are appropriate to manage these risks. IT managers then present this information to senior management. The role of senior management is to allocate resources, specifically money and employees, to prepare for and respond to identified threats and vulnerabilities appropriately.

For this assignment, you assume the role of an IT security manager in a small business. You are tasked with identifying threats and vulnerabilities and determining appropriate risk management techniques.

YieldMore is a small agricultural company that produces and sells fertilizer products. The company headquarters is in a small town in Indiana. Outside its headquarters, there are two large production facilities—one in Nebraska and another in Oklahoma. Furthermore, YieldMore employs salespersons in every state in the United States to serve its customers locally.

The company has three servers located at its headquarters—a Microsoft Active Directory server, an application server, and a database server. The application server hosts YieldMore’s primary software application, which is a proprietary program managing inventory, sales, supply chain, and customer information. The database server manages all data stored locally with direct-attached storage.

All three sites use Ethernet-cabled local area networks (LANs) to connect the users’ Windows 10 workstations via industry-standard managed switches.

The remote production facilities connect headquarters via routers with T-1 LAN connections provided by an external Internet service provider (ISP), and they share an Internet connection through a firewall at headquarters.

Individual salespersons throughout the country connect to YieldMore’s network via virtual private network (VPN) software through their individual Internet connections, typically in a home office.

Assessment approach

Likelihood. Impact. Priority.

This scenario-based assessment uses a 12-month planning horizon. Ratings are conditional estimates under stated assumptions, not measured incident probabilities.

Likelihood

Low (0.10): requires unusual conditions or multiple failures.
Medium (0.50): plausible during the next year.
High (1.00): expected or repeatedly exposed under the stated weak-control assumption.

Business impact

Low (10): limited disruption with routine recovery.
Medium (50): significant interruption to a team or site.
High (100): central-service disruption, major data loss, or broad unauthorized access.

Priority

Likelihood weight × impact score produces a score of 1–100.
1–10: Low; above 10–50: Medium; above 50–100: High.
Ties are resolved by scope of impact and recovery dependencies.

YieldMore example: storage failure is rated Medium likelihood and High impact if no usable independent backup exists. 0.50 × 100 = 50 (Medium). Verifying a tested backup could change the assessment.

Concepts informed by NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 (Joint Task Force Transformation Initiative, 2012). The 3×3 cell values follow the supplied “Balancing Risk and Cost (2 of 2)” course slide. The Low/Medium/High bands are explicitly defined for this project; that slide does not state band cutoffs. The displayed weights are scoring conventions, not measured probabilities for YieldMore.

Understand this assessment

Likelihood asks how plausible the event is. Impact asks how serious its consequences would be. Priority combines them to guide decisions. A low-likelihood event can still deserve attention if the consequences are severe. The same definitions and time horizon apply to every risk.

R4 · Why this rating?

Assume MFA is absent and VPN permissions are broad; verify both. Repeated remote-account exposure supports High likelihood under this assumption.

Business impact: Unauthorized access could reach central business information.

R5 · Why this rating?

Assume endpoints lack applicable extended support and security updates; verify editions, ESU, and patching. Persistent exposure supports High likelihood under this assumption.

Business impact: Compromise could interrupt work and spread if access controls permit it.

R3 · Why this rating?

Assume no tested independent backup is available. Hardware failure or corruption is plausible; no incident history is supplied.

Business impact: Database loss could interrupt inventory, sales, and customer-data access.

R1 · Why this rating?

Assume no independent ISP/firewall failover. Equipment and provider outages are plausible, but frequency is unknown.

Business impact: All sites could lose internet-dependent business services.

R2 · Why this rating?

Assume alternate recovery is unavailable. A major site-disabling event requires less common conditions; power history and protections are unknown.

Business impact: Loss of HQ could remove identity, application, and database services together.

3×3 matrix vs. 3×3×3 McCumber Cube

The risk matrix has two dimensions: three likelihood levels and three impact levels. Its nine cells help compare risk scores. It is the calculator shown above.

The McCumber Cube has three dimensions: security goals (confidentiality, integrity, availability), information states (transmission, storage, processing), and safeguards (technology, policies/practices, human factors). Three choices on each dimension create 27 combinations. It helps check security coverage; it does not calculate this priority score.

YieldMore example: protecting the availability of stored database information through technology leads us to examine backups. Policies define backup and testing responsibilities, and staff training helps people carry them out.

Understand the result

This is an educational comparison tool. A score is a decision aid, not a calculated probability or financial loss estimate. Controls can reduce likelihood, impact, or both. Scores tied in the same band still require judgment about scope, dependencies, cost, and timing.

Risk-handling plan

Match the treatment to the risk.

Recommended treatments are conditional on verifying the assumed gaps. The company does not have to use every treatment category.

R4 · Remote-access account compromise — Mitigate

Require MFA, limit permissions, review remote access, and monitor sign-ins.

Why: Directly addresses account misuse and limits its consequences; stolen sessions remain a residual concern.

R5 · Workstation exploitation — Mitigate

Inventory versions and support; patch and migrate unsupported endpoints, with a temporary supported bridge if needed.

Why: Addresses preventable endpoint exposure; migration needs application compatibility testing.

R3 · Storage failure and data corruption — Mitigate

Create protected independent backups and test restoration against recovery needs.

Why: Reduces recovery consequences; backups must be usable and protected against the same incident.

R1 · Headquarters connectivity outage — Mitigate and share contractual exposure

Evaluate diverse connectivity and firewall failover; use provider service commitments for part of the exposure.

Why: Redundancy reduces outage impact; contracts can share financial exposure but cannot guarantee continuity.

R2 · Headquarters power or site disruption — Mitigate

Verify UPS/generator protection and develop alternate recovery with defined recovery objectives.

Why: Addresses common-location dependency even though the event is rated less likely; remaining downtime needs management acceptance.

Safeguard examples: Center for Internet Security (n.d.), Controls 1, 6, 7, 11, and 12. Treatment concepts: Gibson and Igonor (2022), course reading; verify relevant chapter citations before submission.

Management summary & conclusion

Turn assessment into decisions.

First: verify controls

Resolve unknown safeguards and document the basis for the final risk ratings.

Next: assign and fund

Identify the money, employees, and responsibilities needed for the selected treatments.

Then: test and review

Check whether implemented safeguards reduce the identified risks and address remaining exposure.

Management takeaway: YieldMore’s central services connect the business. Management needs a justified order of action to protect those services and support continued operations.

Understand this slide

Proposed decision: authorize IT to verify authentication, endpoint support, backups, and failover first. Confirmed high risks should receive named owners and a costed implementation plan. No dollar budget or staffing level is assumed from the scenario.

Conclusion

Make time to reduce the risk.

These risks are common in many companies. Identifying them is the first step; making time and assigning responsibility to address them is just as important.
